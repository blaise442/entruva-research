With Gate 5 closed and nothing left to fit, this week was writing. Two
documents exist now, both published. Any later result becomes a new dated
version of each.

The first is the final write-up, assembled from the five gate reports. It
says what the preregistration asked, what passed, what failed, and what each
of the twelve dated deviations was. Every number in it points at a result
file in the repository.

The second is a journal paper for an insurance audience. Its title states the
finding rather than the caveat: telemetry predicts agent failure within
domains but not across them. The abstract carries the pooled pass and the
SWE-bench reversal in the same breath, because a reader who only sees one of
them is misled. The discussion ends with seven requirements an insurer would
need before rating on telemetry, each a number rather than a virtue: loss
labelled outcomes, independent passes in at least three domains, at least 30
held-out agents per domain, a manipulation floor that holds under the stricter
any-agent reading, calibration within 0.05 per decile, the latency family of
signals, and a portability lower bound. This study meets few of them, and the
paper says so. The founder's conflict of interest is declared, and the paper
is written to argue against the company's premise wherever the data does.

Every one of the 22 references was checked against a primary page before it
went in: the publisher, the DOI registry, the arXiv listing or the dataset
record. Where no primary page confirmed a venue, the work is cited as a
preprint.

One thing in the paper is new, though it comes from output we already had.
At Gate 4 we reported that the cheapest manipulation moves no typical agent
a decile but can nudge one held-out agent in ten across a boundary for free.
The question an insurer would ask next is whether that nudge helps the agents
it most needs to catch. Reading the per-agent output again: across all
twenty attack settings there are three downward decile moves in the held-out
set, all from the middle of the distribution. The two worst-scoring agents,
both WebArena agents predicted to fail 93 percent of the time, never move
down under any attack, and the free attacks push their scores up. The
gameable margin is a middle-of-the-distribution effect. The caveat is that
the tail here is two or three agents, so this is evidence about where the
attacks act, not proof that the tail is safe.

The paper is also built as a 17-page PDF with five figures, linked from the
research page. The research directory is now its own public repository with
its full history, so the preregistration, the data manifest, the frozen split,
every result file and the deviations register are linked directly from the
research page, and its README explains the test in plain language and how to
replicate it. Weekly reports continue.
